Today's News English Friday, 20 November 2026

NASA's Voyager 1 phones home from one light-day away

Source: Associated Press, via Google News Top Stories · Date: 19–20 November 2026 · Level: B1–B2 · Reading time: about 6 minutes

New every morning · only this page changes daily · one global hottest story, no categories

What is this story about?

Voyager 1 is a small spacecraft — a machine that flies in space. NASA sent it into space in 1977, almost 50 years ago. Now it is so far from Earth that a radio message takes one whole day to reach it. On Wednesday 18 November 2026, it became the first human-made object ever to be one light-day away from Earth. A light-day is the distance light travels in 24 hours: about 26 billion kilometres (16 billion miles). NASA received its message on Thursday, exactly 24 hours after it was sent.

News excerpt

“NASA's Voyager 1 has phoned home from one light-day away, humanity's first emissary to cross this monumental threshold way out in interstellar space. Flight controllers received the spacecraft's call Thursday, exactly 24 hours after it was sent.”
— Associated Press

Excerpt only — we do not republish the full article. Read the full story at the link at the bottom of this lesson.

Key vocabulary

spacecraft
/ˈspeɪskrɑːft/ · noun

A machine that travels in space. “Voyager 1 is a spacecraft.”

light-day
noun

The distance light travels in one day — about 26 billion kilometres.

signal
noun

A message sent by radio or light. “The signal took 24 hours to arrive.”

interstellar space
noun

The space between the stars, far outside our solar system.

threshold
noun

A point you cross to enter a new place or stage. “It crossed a monumental threshold.”

emissary
noun

A person or thing sent to represent others far away. Here, Voyager 1 represents humanity.

monumental
adjective

Very big and very important.

traverse
verb

To travel across something. “The signal traversed 16 billion miles.”

Key grammar

Present perfect — “has phoned home.”

The news uses the present perfect for a recent event with a result now: has + past participle. Compare: “It phoned home” (past simple — finished time) vs “It has phoned home” (present perfect — the news is new, and it matters now).

Distance as time — “one light-day away.”

English often measures far distances by how long light takes to travel: a light-second, a light-minute, a light-day. “Away” tells you the distance from here.

Exactly — “exactly 24 hours after it was sent.”

“Exactly” makes a number precise. In news writing it shows the fact was checked.

Paragraph structure

  1. Headline — the whole story in one line. “Phones home” is a friendly, human way to say “sent a message back.”
  2. Lead (first paragraph) — who (Voyager 1), what (phoned home from one light-day away), and why it matters (a first for humanity).
  3. Detail — the numbers: 24 hours, 16 billion miles, 26 billion kilometres. Numbers make the scale real.
  4. Background — launched in 1977, now in interstellar space, still sending data after almost 50 years.
  5. What happens next — NASA hopes to stay in touch into the early 2030s, until its power runs too low.

How to read a news article

  1. Read the headline first — it is the story in one sentence.
  2. Read the first paragraph. It answers: who, what, when, where, why.
  3. Look for numbers. They tell you how big, how far, how long.
  4. Look for quotes — words in “…” are what a real person said.
  5. Do not worry about every word. Find the main idea of each paragraph, then go back for the words you need.

Try it yourself

1. How long does a message take to travel from Earth to Voyager 1 now?
2. What does “one light-day” mean?
3. In your own words, why is this story news?

Answers: 1. 24 hours (one day). 2. The distance light travels in 24 hours. 3. Your answer — for example: “Because Voyager 1 is the first human-made object to be that far away.”
